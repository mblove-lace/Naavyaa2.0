Threads of the Earth — The Living Heritage of Indian Cotton

By Naavyaa  ·  2025  ·  8 min read

Home Stories Cotton Heritage

Long before fashion became fast, India dressed herself in cotton — breathable, honest, and alive with the stories of the hands that wove it. Each fold of fabric carried a geography, a season, a prayer.

Cotton is not merely a fibre in India. It is a philosophy. It speaks of the Ganges plains in summer, of artisan communities whose fingers hold centuries of muscle memory, of natural dyes ground from pomegranate rind and indigo leaf. To wear Indian cotton is to wear something that has always belonged to the land.

At Naavyaa, we believe clothing should tell a story worth passing down. Here, we walk you through six extraordinary cotton craft traditions — each one a universe of pattern, purpose, and place.

· · ·
01

Ajrakh

Kutch, Gujarat & Barmer, Rajasthan

Ajrakh is one of India's oldest block-printed textiles — the word derives from the Arabic azrak, meaning blue. Originating with the Khatri community of Kutch, this craft is an act of nature worship as much as textile-making. Fabric is pre-treated with camel dung and castor oil, printed in layers using carved wooden blocks, and washed in the river between each stage.

The characteristic deep indigo and madder red geometric motifs — stars, medallions, interlocking squares — are not merely decorative. They map the cosmos. Artisans say an Ajrakh piece must be complete on both sides, because the sky looks the same from both directions.

After the 2001 Gujarat earthquake devastated Kutch, Ajrakh artisans relocated to Ajrakhpur — a village built specifically to preserve the craft. Today it holds a GI tag and stands as one of India's most globally recognised textile traditions.

02

Bagru

Bagru Village, Rajasthan

Thirty kilometres from Jaipur lies the village of Bagru, where the Chhipa community has practised hand block printing for over 500 years. What makes Bagru distinct is its palette — earthy, warm, grounded. Artisans use natural dyes derived from harda (myrobalan), indigo, and alizarin on cotton pre-treated with a traditional mud-resist called dabu.

Dabu printing is Bagru's signature: a mixture of clay, lime, and gum arabic applied to the fabric before dyeing resists colour in those areas, creating soft muted patterns. The effect is like the texture of weathered walls — deeply human, imperfect, alive.

In Bagru homes, printing is not seasonal work — it is the rhythm of daily life. Fabric is washed in the Sanjaria river that runs through the village, and the courtyard floors double as drying grounds. The whole village breathes with it.

03

Kalamkari

Srikalahasti & Machilipatnam, Andhra Pradesh

Kalam means pen. Kari means work. Kalamkari is literally the art of the pen — hand-drawn or block-printed cotton textiles that tell stories from Hindu mythology, the Ramayana, the Mahabharata. Temple priests once commissioned these enormous narrative cloths to educate worshippers who could not read.

The Srikalahasti style is entirely hand-drawn using a bamboo pen dipped in fermented iron and jaggery solution. Both styles require fabric to pass through up to seventeen treatment stages — myrobalan soaking, cow dung washing, beeswax resist — before a single motif appears.

Every peacock feather, every lotus petal, every royal procession is placed with intention. To wear Kalamkari is to wear a story — one that was told long before books existed.

04

Sanganeri

Sanganer Town, Rajasthan

Named after the town of Sanganer near Jaipur, this style of hand block printing is characterised by delicate floral motifs — butis, creepers, paisleys — in soft fresh tones printed on white or off-white cotton. If Bagru is earthy, Sanganeri is blooming.

Sanganeri printing was historically patronised by the Mughal court, which prized its elegance for garments and furnishings. The white ground of Sanganeri fabric reflects Rajasthani light beautifully, making even simple patterns feel luminous and celebratory.

The wooden blocks — some over a century old — are heirlooms passed father to son. The Chhipa printers wash their fabric in the Saraswati river, whose mineral deposits fix colour naturally. A craft ecosystem in perfect balance with its environment.

05

Hakoba

Maharashtra & Gujarat

Hakoba — also known as eyelet cotton — is a woven fabric defined by its small, evenly spaced holes. The word comes from the Japanese hako (box), describing the square grid of the openwork weave. In India, it became deeply associated with children's clothing and summer wear for its unmatched breathability.

What makes Hakoba remarkable is its simplicity. There is no dye, no print, no embellishment — only the geometry of absence. The holes themselves are the design. Light passes through Hakoba fabric like it passes through a latticed window, creating shadow play on skin.

In recent years, Hakoba has experienced a quiet revival in Indian fashion, worn by those who understand that restraint is its own kind of luxury. A Hakoba blouse in summer is the closest thing to wearing a breeze.

06

Chikankari

Lucknow, Uttar Pradesh

Chikankari is embroidery as meditation. Originating in the royal city of Lucknow under Mughal patronage — legend credits Nur Jahan herself with introducing the craft — it involves hand-stitching intricate white-on-white floral patterns onto muslin or cotton using over thirty-two distinct stitch types.

The work is done entirely by hand, almost entirely by women in the mohallas of Lucknow. A single Chikankari kurta may take several weeks to complete. The stitches — shadow work, phanda, bakhia, jali — each create different textures, some raised, some sunken, some lacy.

Chikankari on cotton is particularly beloved. The soft white thread against cream fabric has a quality photographs cannot capture — it must be held in hand, tilted toward the light, to fully appreciate. It is perhaps the most intimate of India's textile crafts.
